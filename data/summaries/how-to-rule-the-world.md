# How to Rule the World: An Education in Power at Stanford University
*by Theo Baker* | 2026 | Investigative Journalism & Ethics

> **Why it matters:** An explosive inside look at Stanford University’s infamous "Rule the World" power seminar and Silicon Valley’s venture shadow networks. George Polk Award-winning investigative reporter Theo Baker exposes what happens when elite students are systematically trained to treat ethics as a sucker’s bet and power as a pure game of leverage and extraction.

## The Big Idea

For decades, Stanford University has been celebrated as the intellectual beating heart of American technological innovation—the idyllic California incubator that birthed Google, Hewlett-Packard, Sun Microsystems, and OpenAI. But behind the sunny palm trees and terracotta roofs of Palo Alto lies a far more mercenary reality.

In *How to Rule the World*, investigative journalist Theo Baker provides an unflinching account of how Stanford became an uncritical finishing school for an insular global oligarchy. At the center of the book is the secretive, invite-only undergraduate seminar colloquially known on campus as the **"Rule" class** (officially titled *So You Think You Can Rule the World?*). 

In this seminar, hand-picked students—many destined for premier venture capital firms, defense-tech startups, and Washington policy desks—are immersed in the ruthless realpolitik of Peter Thiel, Niccolò Machiavelli, and Bruce Bueno de Mesquita. They are explicitly taught that public morality is theater, that multipolar traps make unilateral ethical restraint suicidal, and that the only metric that matters is constructing monopolistic leverage. 

Baker’s critical counter-read serves as the essential ethical corrective to the entire curriculum. Through investigative journalism, leaked documents, and intimate interviews, Baker demonstrates that when an elite class is trained to view human relationships strictly through payoff matrices and selectorate theory, the result is not visionary leadership. The result is catastrophic institutional decay, sociopathic venture cultures, and personal moral bankruptcy.

## About the Author & Context

Theo Baker is an American investigative journalist who rose to national prominence as a student reporter at *The Stanford Daily*. In 2022–2023, Baker broke a series of investigative exposés documenting widespread scientific research fraud, image manipulation, and data fabrication in peer-reviewed neuroscience papers co-authored by Stanford University President Marc Tessier-Lavigne. 

Baker’s investigative reporting triggered a formal scientific review panel, ultimately forcing Tessier-Lavigne’s historic resignation as president of Stanford in July 2023. At just eighteen years old, Baker became the youngest recipient of the prestigious **George Polk Award in Journalism** in 2023, widely recognized for his fearless commitment to institutional accountability.

Published in 2026, *How to Rule the World* expands Baker’s investigative lens from university governance to the broader ideological machinery of Silicon Valley. Writing as both an embedded insider and a critical observer, Baker deconstructs how elite universities, venture capital networks, and student secret societies forge shadow coalitions that prioritize raw power over democratic accountability and the public good.

## The Breakdown

### Section 1: The "Rule" Seminar and the Classroom as a Crucible

Baker begins by pulling back the curtain on the invite-only seminar that inspired the course. Held in a nondescript conference room in Encina Hall, the class was intentionally kept small—typically 12 to 15 students selected through quiet faculty recommendations and venture scout referrals. 

The syllabus was unapologetic: no soft leadership platitudes, no corporate social responsibility jargon, and no sentimental ethics. Instead, the seminar subjected students to high-stress, real-time crisis simulations:
* **The Whistleblower Suppression Game:** Students were assigned roles as corporate executives dealing with an engineer who discovered critical algorithmic safety flaws. The objective was not to fix the flaw; it was to legally isolate, discredit, and economically silence the engineer before regulatory agencies could intervene.
* **The Boardroom Coup Exercise:** Students role-played selectorate mechanics, identifying the minimum winning coalition on a fictional venture-backed board to strip the founding CEO of super-voting shares and seize control of the cap table.

Baker notes the chilling pedagogical subtext: students were rewarded with high marks and partner introductions at top-tier Sand Hill Road VC firms not for displaying moral courage, but for demonstrating **cold, calculating efficiency**. The implicit lesson was unmistakable: *The world is a multipolar trap. Those who pause to ask ethical questions end up as interchangeables; those who seize the leverage become the essentials.*

A central contribution of Baker’s reporting is mapping what he terms the **Stanford Venture Shadow Network**:

```

  CAMPUS SCOUTS              VENTURE FELLOWSHIPS            CLOSED GATHERINGS

on $10k–$50k retainers    top engineering talent into    in Woodside mansions

```

Baker reveals how major venture capital firms embedded paid undergraduate "scouts" across Stanford’s computer science labs, Greek life houses, and debate clubs. These scouts were incentivized to identify talented students who demonstrated two specific traits:
1. Exceptional technical and mathematical capability.
2. A high degree of **moral pliability**—a willingness to dismiss traditional societal norms, regulatory guardrails, and democratic oversight in pursuit of hyper-growth.

Once identified, students were funneled into private dinners at Woodside and Atherton estates, where prominent tech billionaires reinforced the worldview: *Democracy is slow, broken, and sclerotic; the state is an obstacle; technology founders are the only true sovereigns capable of ruling.*

Baker traces how this subculture directly fostered the hubris behind major Silicon Valley scandals—from the fraudulent biomedical claims of Theranos (founded by Stanford dropout Elizabeth Holmes), to crypto exchange collapses, to the unmonitored deployment of invasive surveillance algorithms.

### Section 3: The Fatal Flaw of Pure Realpolitik

Why does the "Rule the World" philosophy inevitably fail? 

Baker does not deny that game theory and selectorate mechanics describe real-world power dynamics with startling accuracy. His critique is far deeper: **when a descriptive model of power is adopted as a normative lifestyle, it destroys the social capital that makes civilization possible.**

| Dimension | The "Rule" Seminar Mindset | The Real-World Human Fallout |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Relationships** | Strictly transactional; everyone is an interchangeable or a coalition partner. | Paralyzing personal paranoia; inability to form genuine friendships or love. |
| **Institutions** | Systems to be captured, exploited for private goods, or circumvented. | Institutional rot, loss of public trust, and systemic fragility. |
| **Ethics** | An operational vulnerability; a luxury for actors who do not face competition. | Reputational wipeout, federal indictments, and catastrophic blowback. |
| **Long-Term Legacy** | Measured purely by capital retained, moats constructed, and rivals crushed. | A hollowed-out life spent guarding wealth in private bunkers against the public. |

Baker presents heartbreaking profiles of former students who excelled in the seminar:
* Brilliant operators who achieved multi-million dollar payouts by their late twenties, yet found themselves completely isolated, unable to trust their spouses or business partners because they viewed every interaction as an iterated game of potential defection.
* Founders who successfully built corporate monopolies by crushing rivals, only to realize that their entire company culture had adopted the exact same predatory mindset, resulting in internal palace coups and toxic infighting that eventually destroyed the firm from within.


When Baker began investigating allegations of scientific misconduct against Marc Tessier-Lavigne, the university apparatus responded with the exact playbook taught in the power seminars:
* High-powered legal threats were sent to the student newspaper.
* Sophisticated public relations firms were retained to marginalize Baker as a naive college reporter.
* Influential board members and university donors pressured the paper’s advisors to kill the story.

By the logic of selectorate theory, Baker should have been effortlessly crushed. The president controlled a $36 billion endowment and a massive winning coalition; Baker had a laptop and a student press pass.

Why did truth win? Because **transparent verification breaks multipolar coordination traps.** 

When undeniable photographic and forensic evidence of data fabrication was made public, the cost of defending the president became higher than the cost of removing him. The board’s winning coalition fractured in broad daylight. Sunlight remains the ultimate disinfectant against shadow networks of power.

### Section 5: Beyond Ruling: The Stewardship Imperative

Baker concludes with an alternative philosophy of ambition, tailored for the next generation of engineers, founders, and leaders:

1. **Reject the Cynical Fatalism of Moloch:** Just because multipolar traps exist does not mean we must worship them. The greatest historical achievements of humanity—the eradication of smallpox, the establishment of civil rights, the creation of the internet itself—were achieved not by maximizing extraction, but by building robust institutional coordination mechanisms.
2. **Build Democratic and Institutional Resilience:** Real power does not consist of insulating yourself with a microscopic winning coalition; it consists of building institutions that are resilient, transparent, and accountable to the broad public.
3. **Moral Courage Is Not Weakness:** In an ecosystem saturated with cynical, transactional operators, **integrity is the ultimate contrarian moat.** A leader whose word is genuinely trustworthy, who refuses to sacrifice subordinates for short-term gain, and who measures success by public goods rather than private extraction becomes an extraordinary magnet for world-class talent.

## Key Takeaways & Actionable Framework

1. **Beware the Seduction of Cynicism:** It is easy to confuse cynicism with sophistication. Dismissing human ethics as mere theater does not make you a master strategist; it blinds you to the vital power of public trust, social capital, and moral legitimacy.
2. **Accountability Is the Ultimate Antidote to Shadow Power:** When powerful actors operate behind closed doors, shine a light on their incentives. Transparent public evidence shatters corrupt coalitions faster than any political counter-coup.
3. **Culture Replicates Your Methodology:** If you build your company or organization through cutthroat defection and transactional deception, your subordinates will use those exact same tools against you. You will inevitably be devoured by the machine you constructed.
4. **Choose Stewardship Over Domination:** The measure of a truly educated mind is not whether you can figure out how to rule the world, but whether you possess the wisdom, humility, and moral discipline to leave it better than you found it.
